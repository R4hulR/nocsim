\begin{abstract}
A 2D torus gives every router in an on-chip network four neighbours, but it
pays for that regularity with wraparound links that run the full width of the
die. In earlier undergraduate work we proposed a ``modified torus'' that keeps
the corner wraparounds and folds the remaining boundary links back onto the
same edge, which shortens them. That evaluation looked only at hop counts on a
single $5\times5$ grid. Here we re-examine the design properly. We give a rule
that extends it to any $n\times n$ grid, measure physical wire length and
channel load, and simulate it cycle by cycle against a mesh and a torus. At
$8\times8$ the modified torus uses 21\% less wire than a torus and has about the
same average hop count, but its saturation throughput under uniform traffic is
45\% lower, close to that of a mesh. It does better than the torus under
tornado traffic (+50\%) and much worse under bit-complement. We also show that
the shortest-path BFS routing used originally deadlocks at 20\% load, and we
replace it with adaptive routing over an up*/down* escape channel. Finally, we
treat the placement of boundary links as a search problem. Simulated annealing,
REINFORCE and random search, given the same evaluation budget, find no layout
that beats the hand design at its own wire budget. They do trace out a steep
wire/throughput frontier between it and the torus.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

On-chip networks replaced shared buses once chips had too many cores for a
single bus to serve~\cite{dally2001route,kumar2002noc}. The 2D mesh is the
default topology because it maps directly onto a tiled floorplan and every link
is one tile long. Its weakness is diameter: a packet may need $2(n-1)$ hops
on an $n\times n$ mesh. The torus halves the diameter by adding a wraparound
link to every row and column. On a flat floorplan, though, each wraparound is
$n-1$ tiles long. Long links need repeaters or extra pipeline stages, and they
take up routing tracks~\cite{dally2004principles}.

Our undergraduate thesis~\cite{thesis2023} proposed a compromise. Keep the
torus wraparounds at the four corners, and link most other boundary routers to
another router on the \emph{same} edge, about half an edge away. Every router
still has four ports, and most of the extra links are roughly half as long. The
thesis reported that this ``modified torus'' beats both the mesh and the torus.
However, it measured only BFS hop counts on a hand-written $5\times5$ adjacency
list. It had no notion of wire length, contention or deadlock. Its
``throughput'' was the reciprocal of hop count.

This paper is the evaluation that the original design should have had. The
contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item A construction rule that extends the topology to any $n\geq5$. It
        reproduces the original $5\times5$ list exactly and matches the
        thesis figures up to $10\times10$ (Section~\ref{sec:topology}).
  \item A comparison of mesh, torus and modified torus on wire length, hop
        count and channel-load throughput for $n=5,\dots,10$, followed by
        cycle-level simulation under four traffic patterns
        (Sections~\ref{sec:static} and~\ref{sec:sim}).
  \item A demonstration that the original routing deadlocks, both from its
        channel dependency graph and in simulation, and a deadlock-free
        replacement that works on any topology (Section~\ref{sec:deadlock}).
  \item A formulation of boundary-link placement as a matching problem, with a
        comparison of random search, simulated annealing and policy-gradient
        reinforcement learning on it (Section~\ref{sec:search}).
\end{itemize}

The headline result is a trade-off, not a win. The modified torus saves wire and
keeps the torus's low zero-load latency, but it gives up roughly half of the
torus's saturation throughput. Within that trade-off the hand design holds up
well. None of our search methods found a better layout with the same amount of
wire.

The simulator, experiments and all raw results are available as open-source
code.\footnote{[repository URL]}

\section{Background}

\textbf{Topologies.} A $k$-ary 2-cube (torus) has diameter $k$ for even $k$
and, under uniform traffic with load evenly balanced, saturates at an injection
rate of $8/k$ packets per node per cycle~\cite{dally2004principles}. The mesh
saturates at about $4/k$. Folded tori interleave routers so that every link has
the same length, at the cost of doubling the length of the short
links~\cite{dally2004principles}. Express virtual channels~\cite{kumar2007evc} take a
different approach: packets bypass the router pipelines of intermediate nodes
on a mesh, which cuts the per-hop cost of long trips. The flattened
butterfly~\cite{kim2007flattened} instead adds long links with high-radix
routers. The modified torus stays at radix 4
and only changes \emph{where} the boundary links go.

\textbf{Deadlock.} With finite buffers, packets can wait on each other in a
cycle. Dally and Seitz showed that deterministic routing is deadlock-free if
and only if its channel dependency graph (CDG) is acyclic~\cite{dally1987deadlock}.
Shortest-path routing on a ring or torus produces cyclic CDGs. Duato's theory
allows fully adaptive routing as long as an acyclic \emph{escape} sub-network
is always reachable~\cite{duato1993theory}. Up*/down* routing, introduced for
the Autonet LAN~\cite{schroeder1991autonet}, provides such an escape network on
any connected graph.

\textbf{Search.} We compare simulated annealing~\cite{kirkpatrick1983sa} with
REINFORCE~\cite{williams1992reinforce}. Reinforcement learning has been used
for NoC \emph{routing} since Q-routing~\cite{boyan1994qrouting}. Here we use it
for topology layout.

\section{The Topology and Its Design Space}
\label{sec:topology}

Number the routers of an $n\times n$ grid by (row, column). On a mesh, each
corner router has two unused ports and every other boundary router has one,
giving $4n$ spare ports in total. Any perfect matching of these ports adds $2n$
links and leaves every router with radix 4. The torus is the matching that pairs
each port with the opposite edge.

\textbf{Construction.} The modified torus pairs the ports as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Each corner keeps both torus wraparounds.
  \item If $n$ is odd, the middle row and middle column keep their wraparounds.
  \item On each edge, the remaining positions strictly between the corners are
        listed in order and split into two halves of equal size. Position $i$
        of the first half is linked to position $i$ of the second half.
\end{enumerate}
Each such link spans $\lfloor (n-1)/2\rfloor$ positions along its edge, so it is
about half as long as a wraparound. For $n=5$ the rule gives exactly the 50
links in the original hand-written code. For $n=6$ to $10$ it matches the
figures in the thesis. Fig.~\ref{fig:topologies} shows the $8\times8$ case. For
$n=4$ the folded links would duplicate mesh links, so the topology is defined
only for $n\geq5$.

\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Analytical metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}

Wire length is the Manhattan distance between the two routers of a link,
measured in tile pitches, with no folding. For throughput we compute channel
loads with each flow split evenly over all of its minimal paths. Let $d(s,t)$
be the hop distance, $\sigma_{st}$ the number of shortest $s\to t$ paths, and
$T_{st}$ the fraction of node $s$'s traffic sent to $t$. The load on directed
channel $c=(u\to v)$ per unit injection rate is
\begin{equation}
  \gamma_c = \sum_{s,t} T_{st}\,\frac{\sigma_{su}\,\sigma_{vt}}{\sigma_{st}}
  \,\mathbf{1}\!\left[d(s,u)+1+d(v,t)=d(s,t)\right],
\end{equation}
and the throughput estimate is
\begin{equation}
  \hat\Theta = \Big(\max\big\{\max_c \gamma_c,\; \max_t \textstyle\sum_s T_{st}\big\}\Big)^{-1},
\end{equation}
where the second term is the ejection limit of one packet per router per
cycle. This is an estimate rather than a bound. Adaptive routing can balance
load better than an even split, and real routers lose some bandwidth to
contention. For a torus the formula reproduces the textbook $8/k$
result~\cite{dally2004principles}, up to a factor of $(N-1)/N$ because we
exclude self-traffic, and our test suite checks this.

\subsection{Routing and deadlock}
\label{sec:routing}

We implement three routing functions. \emph{BFS} follows one fixed shortest
path, with ties broken by neighbour index; this is the original algorithm.
\emph{Up*/down*} builds a BFS tree from the most central router and only
allows paths that go zero or more hops up the tree and then zero or more hops
down. \emph{Adaptive} lets packets on virtual channels (VCs) $1..V{-}1$ take
any minimal next hop, choosing the one whose downstream buffer has the most
free space. A packet that finds every minimal option full drops into VC~0,
where it follows up*/down*. Because buffers hold whole packets, it may move
back to an adaptive VC at any later hop. We build the CDG of each routing
function and check it for cycles with a standard graph algorithm. For adaptive
routing we check the CDG of the escape network, which is what Duato's
condition requires.

\subsection{Cycle-level simulator}
\label{sec:simulator}

We wrote a cycle-level simulator in Python (under 400 lines), modelled on the
methodology of~\cite{dally2004principles}. Tools such as BookSim~\cite{jiang2013booksim}
or Garnet~\cite{agarwal2009garnet} would also work. We wanted irregular,
searched topologies and the escape scheme above, and a small simulator we could
read end to end was easier to verify. Each router is input-queued, with $V=3$
VCs per port and 4-packet buffers per VC, and uses credit-based flow control.
Each cycle, each output port and each input port forwards at most one packet,
and the allocator rotates its starting input for fairness. A hop costs one
router cycle plus the link delay. Links take one cycle in the default model.
In a second ``wire'' model, a link of length $L$ takes $\lceil L/2\rceil$
cycles, so long wraparounds pay for their length. Packets are single flits
with virtual cut-through.

Traffic is open-loop Bernoulli injection into unbounded source queues. Each run
warms up for 1000 cycles, tags packets created during the next 3000, and runs
until all tagged packets have been delivered. A point counts as saturated if
tagged packets are still in flight 6000 cycles after the window, or if the
network delivered less than 95\% of the offered load. We report saturation
throughput as the highest offered load that is not saturated and whose latency
stays below three times the zero-load latency. A run counts as deadlocked if
packets are in the network but none moves for 1000 cycles.

\subsection{Topology search}
\label{sec:searchmethod}

The design space is the set of valid matchings of the $4n$ spare boundary
ports: no self-loops, no duplicates of mesh links, and no duplicate links. We
maximise
\begin{multline}
  J(G) = \frac{1}{|P|}\sum_{p\in P}\frac{\hat\Theta_p(G)}{\hat\Theta_p(\text{torus})}
  - 0.25\,\frac{\bar h(G)}{\bar h(\text{torus})} \\
  - 5\,\frac{\max(0,\,W(G)-B)}{B},
\end{multline}
where $P$ is the set \{uniform, transpose, bit-complement, tornado\},
$\bar h$ is the mean hop count, $W$ is total wire length and $B$ is the wire
budget. The penalty term steers the search back to feasible designs rather than
forbidding infeasible ones outright.

\emph{Random search} samples matchings uniformly by shuffling the ports and
rejecting invalid pairings. \emph{Simulated annealing} starts from a random or a
given matching. Its move picks two links $(a,b),(c,d)$ and re-pairs them as
$(a,c),(b,d)$ or $(a,d),(b,c)$, which keeps every router at radix 4. The
temperature cools geometrically from 0.05 to 0.001. \emph{REINFORCE} builds a
matching one port at a time. At each step it takes the lowest-numbered
unmatched port $i$ and samples a partner $j$ from a softmax over learned logits
$\theta_{ij}$, masked to valid partners and mixed with 2\% uniform exploration.
It trains with a batch-mean baseline and advantages normalised by the batch
standard deviation (batch 64, learning rate 8). An episode
that reaches a dead end gets a fixed low reward. All three methods get 2500
distinct objective evaluations, and repeated designs are served from a cache
for free.

Table~\ref{tab:static} and Fig.~\ref{fig:static} summarise the analytical
comparison. The original claim about hop count holds, but only just. The
modified torus is within 6\% of the torus at every size from 5 to 10. It is
slightly better at most sizes, the largest gain being 5.9\% at $9\times9$, and
1.7\% worse at $8\times8$. The wire saving is real and grows with $n$: 10\%
at $5\times5$, 21\% at $8\times8$ and 22\% at $10\times10$.

Throughput is where the two designs part ways. In a torus every channel carries
the same load under uniform traffic. In the modified torus, traffic that would
have crossed the die on a wraparound is pushed onto the short folded links and
the edge routers around them, and those become the bottleneck. At $5\times5$
the effect is mild (0.95 against 1.00), but by $9\times9$ the estimate is below
that of a plain mesh (0.28 against 0.33). The original evaluation, which
measured throughput as $1/\text{hops}$, could not have seen this.

\subsection{Deadlock}
\label{sec:deadlock}

The CDG of BFS routing is cyclic on both the torus and the modified torus. On
the mesh it happens to be acyclic, because breaking ties toward the lower
neighbour index acts like a turn restriction. The up*/down* escape network is
acyclic on all three.

Simulation agrees with the CDG analysis (Table~\ref{tab:deadlock}). With a
single VC, BFS routing ran cleanly up to 15\% load and then deadlocked in every
run at 20\% and above, typically within a few hundred cycles. The adaptive
scheme never deadlocked, including at injection rates of 0.6 and 1.0, far past
saturation. Every simulation result below uses it.

Table~\ref{tab:sim} and Fig.~\ref{fig:latency} show the simulated behaviour.
The modified torus behaves like a torus at low load and like a mesh near
saturation. Its zero-load latency under uniform traffic is 8.3 cycles, against
8.1 for the torus and 10.6 for the mesh. Its saturation throughput is 0.40,
the same as the mesh and 45\% below the torus. The ordering under transpose is
the same.

The traffic pattern matters a great deal. Under tornado traffic, where every
node sends to the node $\lceil n/2\rceil-1$ columns to its right (wrapping
around), the modified
torus saturates at 0.37 against 0.25 for the torus. We suspect this is
because the folded links on the top and bottom rows span three columns, exactly
the distance tornado traffic travels at $n=8$, but we have not analysed it
further. Bit-complement is the opposite case. Every packet has to cross the
centre of the chip, the folded links run along the edges and do little for that
traffic, and the modified torus saturates at 0.10, below even the mesh.

Charging long links their real delay (bottom row of Fig.~\ref{fig:latency})
moves the zero-load results in the expected direction. The modified torus
becomes slightly faster than the torus at low load: 9.4 against 9.6 cycles for
uniform traffic and 10.2 against 10.7 for transpose. These gains are small, and
saturation throughput is almost unchanged.

The simulator generally achieves 70--95\% of the analytical estimate
$\hat\Theta$. The torus, at 74\% under uniform traffic, is at the low end.
Adaptive routing occasionally exceeds the estimate (mesh and modified torus
under tornado). This is why we treat $\hat\Theta$ only as a search heuristic and
check its conclusions in simulation.

At the modified torus's own budget of 176 tiles, no method found a better
layout (Table~\ref{tab:search}, Fig.~\ref{fig:convergence}). Annealing from
random starts came closest, at $J=0.34$ against 0.37. Annealing started
\emph{from} the modified torus never found anything better than its starting
point. REINFORCE came out
between random search and annealing. We think the main reason is that its
policy is tabular. It learns a separate logit for every pair of ports and has
no way to see that two pairings are geometrically similar, so 2500 episodes are
not enough. A policy that shares parameters across positions, such as a graph
network over the floorplan, would be the obvious next step.

Fig.~\ref{fig:frontier} sweeps the budget. The best designs found trace a
frontier that the modified torus sits on, and it is steep near the torus.
Going from 208 to 224 tiles (8\% more wire) raises the throughput estimate from
0.71 to 1.0 of the torus's. At 224 tiles the best design found is the torus
itself, reached by annealing started from it; cold starts did not get there.
Below 176 tiles the designs found have fewer hops than a mesh but lower
throughput. This is a limit of the design space, which requires every spare port to be used, so
leaving ports idle would sometimes be the better choice.

\begin{figure*}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{searched_topology.png}
  \caption{The modified torus (left), the best 208-tile design found by
  annealing (centre), and the torus (right).}
  \label{fig:searched}

The 208-tile design (Fig.~\ref{fig:searched}) is a useful intermediate point.
It is irregular and uses 7\% less wire than the torus. It has the lowest
zero-load latency of the four networks (7.5 cycles uniform, 6.7 transpose) and
twice the bit-complement throughput of the modified torus. It is still well
short of the torus on saturation throughput (Table~\ref{tab:sim}).

One lesson from the search concerns the objective. Our first version optimised
the uniform-traffic estimate alone. At 176 tiles it returned a layout with a
13\% higher estimate than the modified torus, which looked like a clear
improvement. In a preliminary simulation, that layout was indeed better on
bit-complement (0.18 against 0.10), but it was 16\% worse on transpose. Scoring
designs on a mix of traffic patterns removed the problem, and with it the
apparent improvement.

\section{Limitations}

Our wire model is the Manhattan length on a flat floorplan. A folded layout
would change the comparison with the torus, and we have no area or power model
beyond counting wire tiles. The simulator uses single-flit packets and a simple
greedy allocator, so absolute numbers should not be compared with
wormhole-switched results from BookSim or Garnet. We have checked it against
closed-form torus results and against our own analytical estimates, but not
against those tools. All traffic is synthetic. Whether the modified torus is
worth building depends on whether real workloads look more like tornado or
like bit-complement, and that needs application traces. Finally, the search
covers only boundary-link layouts at radix 4 on an $8\times8$ grid.

\section{Conclusion}

The modified torus does what it was designed to do. It removes a fifth of the
torus's wire without increasing the hop count. Its original evaluation missed
the cost: about half of the torus's saturation throughput, and a routing
algorithm that deadlocks. The design has a clear niche, tornado-like traffic
and wire-constrained floorplans, and at its own wire budget neither annealing
nor reinforcement learning found a better layout. The broader lesson is a
methodological one. Hop counts alone, or any single cheap objective, can make a
design look better than it is, and the conclusions changed each time we added
a more realistic measurement.
